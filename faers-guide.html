FAERS / AEMS data guide

Spontaneous adverse-event reports are valuable for post-market safety surveillance, but they are not a complete census of side effects. This guide explains what PharmaSafe is actually reading when it queries the FDA data through openFDA.

What is FAERS?

The FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) contains adverse-event and medication-error reports submitted to the FDA. FDA uses the database as part of post-marketing safety surveillance for drugs and therapeutic biologics. FDA is also implementing the broader Adverse Event Monitoring System (AEMS), which is intended to consolidate reporting systems across FDA-regulated product categories.

PharmaSafe's drug-event workflows use the public drug adverse-event data exposed through openFDA. The API converts source records into a machine-readable format and supports searches and frequency counts.

What a report can contain

A public safety report can include general report information, patient information, products, reactions, and other fields. One report may list multiple drugs and multiple reactions. That structure matters: the presence of a drug and a reaction in the same report does not establish that the named drug caused that reaction.

Report volume

A count is a count of submitted reports returned by the data source. It is not an incidence rate and does not automatically account for how many people used a product.

Reporting is incomplete

Not every adverse event is reported. Reporting behavior can change with awareness, publicity, product age, regulatory activity, and other factors.

Duplicate or updated reports

Safety reporting systems can receive follow-up or updated submissions. A case identifier and report structure therefore need to be interpreted carefully rather than treated as a simple patient count.

Data freshness

The openFDA drug adverse-event dataset is updated periodically and may lag the underlying FDA reporting activity. PharmaSafe shows the data it can retrieve; it does not invent newer records.

Why PharmaSafe uses screening language

PharmaSafe deliberately calls its ROR and PRR results statistical screening signals. A disproportionality result can help a researcher decide which drug–reaction pair deserves further investigation, but it does not prove causality, incidence, or clinical risk for an individual patient.
