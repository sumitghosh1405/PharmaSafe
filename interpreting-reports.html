How to interpret an adverse-event report

A safety report is a record of something reported to a regulator, not a laboratory experiment. Reading it well means separating what the record explicitly contains from conclusions that require additional evidence.

Start with the report, not the conclusion

Look at the report identifier, receive date, patient fields when available, listed products, reactions, and outcomes. Then ask what the record actually establishes. If several products and reactions appear together, the database does not automatically tell you which product caused which reaction.

Reaction count

Reaction counts tell you how many returned reports contain a reaction term under the query conditions. They do not provide population incidence.

Outcome

Outcome fields describe what was reported, such as hospitalization or death. They do not by themselves establish the cause of the outcome.

Receive date

The receive date is part of the reporting record. Trends can reflect both underlying events and changes in reporting behavior.

Case identifier

The public safety report identifier lets a researcher reopen a specific record in the report viewer when it is available through the public dataset.

Common interpretation traps

  • Equating report count with incidence.
  • Assuming the most frequently reported reaction is necessarily the most dangerous reaction.
  • Assuming temporal association proves causality.
  • Ignoring co-medications, underlying disease, age, or other patient and product context.
  • Treating a single report or a statistical signal as clinical advice.

Use the right next step

For research, a report can be a starting point for case review, signal investigation, label checking, literature review, or a more formal epidemiologic study. For personal medical decisions, the appropriate next step is consultation with a qualified healthcare professional and review of authoritative product information.

The FDA explains that adverse-event reports cannot by themselves establish a causal relationship and cannot be used to estimate incidence. See the openFDA overview.
